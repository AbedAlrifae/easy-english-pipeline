Yusuf opened the shop at eight in the morning, the same as every other day.
The shop was small and it was full of old machines.
There were machines on every table and on the floor, under a soft gray dust.
His father had opened this shop forty years ago, on a narrow street near the market.
The street had changed around him without asking.
The bread shop had become a phone shop. The phone shop had become a place that sold coffee.
Yusuf's window was the same window, with the same gold letters, getting older every year.
Back then, people brought things to be fixed because a new one cost too much.
Now people did not fix things at all.
They put the broken thing in a bag and they bought a new one that evening.
Yusuf had three customers in a good week.
On Monday a man had come in with a radio from his mother's house.
Yusuf opened it and found one loose wire and nothing else wrong.
The man watched him fix it in four minutes and then asked what it was worth.
Nothing, Yusuf said. You can have it. It works now.
The man looked at the radio for a while.
Then he said he had already bought a new one.
He left the old one on the counter and went out into the street.
It was still on the shelf behind the door, working, waiting for nobody.
He spent most of his days alone with a small tool and the radio playing low.
His father was very old now and stayed at home, in a chair by the window.
Every Sunday Yusuf visited him, and every Sunday his father asked the same question.
Is the shop busy.
And every Sunday Yusuf said yes, it is busy, because the truth was heavy.

On a Tuesday in October a woman came in at four in the afternoon.
She was maybe fifty years old and she carried a plastic bag with both hands.
She put the bag on the counter very carefully.
She put it down the way you put down something that is sleeping.
Inside there was a cassette player, the kind with two big buttons and a handle.
The plastic had turned yellow. One corner was cracked.
My name is Nadia, she said. Can you make this work.
Yusuf opened the lid. There was a cassette inside, and the cassette had no label.
Someone had written nothing on it at all, not a name, not a date.
He asked her what was on it.
Nadia looked at the wall behind him for a long moment.
I do not know, she said. That is the problem.
He waited, but she did not say anything more.
So he wrote her number on a small card and told her he would try.

That night Yusuf worked until the street outside went quiet.
The motor turned, but it turned too slowly.
The sound came out low and wrong, like a voice under water.
He cleaned every part he could reach. He changed the belt. It did not help.
The problem was a small metal wheel inside, and that wheel was worn down flat.
He searched the internet for two hours.
The company had closed in 1994 and nobody sold that part any more.
He found one shop in another country with a photo of it, but the photo was from 2011.
He sent a message. Nobody answered.
For three days the yellow machine sat on his table.
He walked past it every morning and every evening and he felt small.
He had told a woman he would try, and trying was not going to be enough.
On the fourth morning he decided to call her and say no.
He practiced the sentence twice before he picked up the phone.
I am sorry, he said. The part does not exist any more. I cannot do it.
Nadia was quiet for a moment on the other end of the line.
Then she said, my father kept that machine in a box for thirty years.
He never played the tape either. He only kept it.
I think he was waiting until he had time, she said.
Then she said she understood, and she said thank you, and she put the phone down.
Yusuf stood in the shop with the phone still in his hand.
He looked at the yellow machine on the table by the window.
He did not call her back that day.
But he did not put the machine away either.

On Sunday he sat with his father by the window.
This time he did not say that the shop was busy.
Instead he described the machine, and the flat little wheel, and the cassette with no label.
His father listened with his eyes closed, and Yusuf thought he had fallen asleep.
Then the old man said, go to the back room. The blue box on the second shelf.
Yusuf found the box under a pile of old newspapers. It was heavier than he expected.
Inside there were hundreds of small parts in paper bags, and each bag had a date on it.
His father had kept every part from every machine he had ever taken apart.
Forty years of small metal things that somebody might need one day.
He sat on the floor of the back room with the paper bags spread around him.
It took Yusuf an hour to find it. The bag said March 1989.
In 1989 his father had been the age that Yusuf was now.
Yusuf tried to imagine him taking a machine apart on a slow afternoon.
He tried to imagine him holding one small wheel and deciding it was worth keeping.
The wheel inside was the right size. It had almost no wear on it.
He showed it to his father. The old man only moved his head, as if this were normal.
That is why you keep things, he said. Not because you need them. Because someone will.

Yusuf fitted the wheel on Monday morning and the motor turned at the right speed.
He called Nadia and she came within the hour, still in her work clothes.
They stood together at the counter and neither of them touched the button.
Do you want to listen here, he asked, or do you want to take it home.
Here, she said. If it is nothing, I would rather it be nothing here.
So he pressed play.
There was a long soft noise of empty tape, and then a kitchen.
A woman was singing quietly, not for anyone.
She was singing the way people sing when they are cooking and have forgotten the door is open.
A pan was moving in the kitchen. A boy was complaining about his homework.
A radio was playing in another room, a different song, too far away to name.
Somebody opened a window and the street came in for a moment, cars and a bird.
That was all. It went on for twenty minutes and nothing happened in it at all.
Yusuf wanted to leave the room and let her have it alone.
But she had said here, and so he stayed behind the counter and looked at his hands.
The singing stopped for a while and then started again in the middle of a line.
Nadia stood very still with one hand flat on the counter.
It was my mother, she said. That is our old kitchen.
My father used to leave the recorder on and then forget about it.
He recorded nothing. Just Tuesdays.
We sold the house in the spring and I have been trying to remember the sound of it.
I could remember the rooms. I could remember the light in the afternoon.
I could not remember the sound, and the sound was the part I wanted.
She listened to the whole cassette twice and she paid him and she said thank you.
Then she carried the yellow machine out with both hands.
She carried it out the same careful way she had brought it in.

Yusuf sat in the empty shop for a long time after she left.
He thought about his father in the chair by the window, asking if the shop was busy.
He thought about the blue box, and the forty years of small parts, and March 1989.
He thought about how many people came into the shop to throw something away.
They did not call it that. They called it asking for a price.
But he knew the difference now.
He had known it for years without having a word for it.
He thought about all the things in his own life that made a sound.
The bell over the door. The clock in his father's hall. His father saying his name.
None of it was written down anywhere, and none of it was going to wait for him.
The next Sunday he brought an old recorder with him and an empty cassette.
He did not tell his father what he was doing.
He put it on the table between them and pressed the button and let it run.
They talked about nothing at all. The price of tea.
A neighbor who had painted his front door green and would not say why.
His father told a story about a customer in 1981 who paid him with a chicken.
In the background the clock in the hall was doing what it always did.
When the hour was finished, Yusuf did not write anything on the cassette.
He put it in a box under the counter at the shop, next to the blue one.
He is not ready to listen to it yet.
But it is there, and one day he will be ready.
And the machine that plays it still works, because somebody kept the part.
